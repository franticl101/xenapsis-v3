Most writing about personality tests falls into one of two camps: uncritical enthusiasm, or a dismissal that treats the whole field as astrology. Both are wrong, and the second is wrong in a way that costs people something useful.

Here is the defensible middle.

## What "accurate" actually means

Two different things get collapsed into the word accurate, and separating them resolves most of the argument.

**Reliability** asks whether the test gives you the same answer twice. If you take an instrument today and again in five weeks, does it report the same thing?

**Validity** asks whether the thing it reports corresponds to anything real — whether knowing your result tells anyone anything they could not have guessed.

A test can be highly reliable and completely invalid. It can consistently sort you into the same meaningless box every time.

## Where type-based tests do reasonably well

The four dimensions underlying the Myers-Briggs tradition are not arbitrary. Three of them map onto dimensions that appear independently in the Big Five, which is the most empirically supported model in personality research:

- Extraversion–Introversion maps closely onto Big Five extraversion
- Sensing–Intuition maps onto openness to experience
- Thinking–Feeling maps onto agreeableness
- Judging–Perceiving maps onto conscientiousness

So the *questions* are measuring something. People's answers cluster in ways that are stable and meaningful, and self-reported preferences do correlate with observable behaviour.

## Where they fall down

**The binary letter throws away most of the information.** This is the single biggest problem, and it is a design decision rather than a measurement failure. The underlying scores are continuous and roughly normally distributed — most people are somewhere in the middle of each dimension, not at the edges. Forcing that into a letter means someone at 51% and someone at 94% receive the same letter and are described identically.

That is also why people get different results from different tests. If you sit near the midpoint, your letter is close to a coin toss. It is not that one test is wrong; it is that the question "which side are you on" has no stable answer for you.

This is why [our personality type test](/tests/personality-type/) reports the percentage first and the letter second, and says plainly when a result is a near tie.

**Test-retest reliability is weak where it matters.** Studies have found a substantial proportion of people receive a different four-letter type when retested weeks later. Almost all of that instability comes from dimensions where the person scored near the midpoint — which is most people, on at least one dimension.

**Predictive validity for job performance is poor.** This is the claim to be most sceptical of. Type does not meaningfully predict how well someone will do a job. The Myers-Briggs Company itself states the instrument should not be used for hiring or selection. Anyone selling you type-based recruitment screening is overselling.

**The descriptions are written to be agreeable.** Type profiles tend to describe strengths in flattering language and weaknesses as strengths in disguise — "your perfectionism comes from caring so much". A description vague and positive enough will feel accurate to almost anyone, a tendency known as the Barnum effect. This is a property of how profiles are usually *written*, not an inherent limit of the instrument, which is why the profiles on this site state blind spots plainly and lead with the stress description rather than the strengths list.

## What the results are genuinely good for

Strip out the overclaiming and something useful remains.

**Describing working conditions.** Type predicts job *performance* weakly and job *satisfaction* moderately. It is poor at telling you what you could do and considerably better at telling you what would wear you down — interruption, isolation, pace, politics, whether the work has a visible point. Most people who leave a role leave over conditions, not capability.

**Supplying vocabulary.** A great deal of the value is simply having language for something you already knew. "I need an hour alone after a long meeting to be useful again" is a more actionable sentence than a vague sense of being tired, and it is one you can say to a manager.

**Explaining friction with other people.** The most reliably useful application is recognising that a colleague who needs the full reasoning before agreeing is not being obstructive, and a colleague who decides fast is not being reckless. They are weighting different information.

## How to read your own result

1. **Look at the percentage before the letter.** Anything inside roughly 55% is a near tie. Treat that dimension as "both, depending on context" rather than as a trait.
2. **Test the stress description, not the strengths.** Strengths lists feel true for nearly everyone. The specific way a type falls apart under pressure is precise enough to be wrong — and if it is wrong, you probably have a different type.
3. **Disbelieve any prediction about ability.** If a result tells you that you cannot do something, that is the result exceeding its evidence.
4. **Use it as a hypothesis.** The right response is "does this describe me?", not "this is what I am".
